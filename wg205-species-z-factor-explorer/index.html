Species richness vs. area
  • Species richness (S) generally increases as the area (A) surveyed increases, but not without limit — the relationship curves and eventually flattens rather than rising forever.
  • This relationship is expressed as S = C·AZ, where C is a constant and Z is the slope (the regression coefficient) of the relationship on a logarithmic scale.
  • On a normal (linear scale), the species-area relationship appears as a curve — specifically a rectangular hyperbola.
  • On a log-log scale, the same relationship becomes a straight line, described by log S = log C + Z log A, with Z as the line's slope and log C as its y-intercept.
  • Ecologists have found that Z usually lies between 0.1 and 0.2, regardless of the taxonomic group or the region studied.
  • When the species-area relationship is examined across very large areas, such as entire continents, the slope Z is much steeper, typically in the range of 0.6 to 1.2.
  • A steeper Z means species richness increases much more sharply with area; a shallower Z means richness rises more gradually and flattens sooner.
